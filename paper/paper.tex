\subsubsection*{Background}
Meiotic recombination during gamete formation is a fundamental evolutionary process. It is required to ensure proper chromosome segregation and promotes genetic diversity by shuffling alleles into novel haplotypes. This can create beneficial allele combinations and break up linkage between beneficial and harmful alleles, thereby facilitating adaptive evolution. However, high recombination rates also carry risks, such as introducing mutations at crossover sites, and potentially disrupting favorable allele combinations. Recombination rates could thus affect individual fitness in opposite directions, suggesting a complex relationship with individual fitness.

Investigating the impact of recombination rates on fitness is complicated by the fact that identifying crossover positions during meiosis - and thus finding the crossover count - is only possible for individuals with genotyped offspring. This limitation introduces a sampling bias which prevents measuring the recombination rates of individuals that are reproductively unsuccessful, excluding them from potential fitness analyses. Failure to account for these unmeasured individuals could potentially bias analyses of the fitness effects of recombination rates. 

\subsubsection*{Materials and methods}
We used a long-term house sparrow study system from northern Norway with extensive pedigree, genotype, and fitness data to investigate the relationship between individual recombination rates and fitness components. The genomic and pedigree data were leveraged to measure autosomal crossover count (ACC) in adult house sparrows, resulting in one ACC measure per genotyped parent-offspring pair. We estimated sex-specific individual genetic values for ACC by using the phenotypic ACC measures for each parent as responses in a genomic prediction model. The genomic prediction, implemented as a Bayesian G-BLUP model, allowed us to estimate these genetic values in more than 2,600 phenotyped individuals, as well as in more than 2,900 additional individuals with no recorded offspring, correcting the aforementioned sampling bias and increasing the sample size of the subsequent fitness analyses.

We then related the estimated genetic values to sex-specific fitness components, including annual reproductive success and annual survival. As the individuals without ACC measurements had higher uncertainty in their estimated genetic values, we used measurement error modeling to account for the heterogeneous genetic value uncertainties. 

\subsubsection*{Results}
We found that individual ACC was related to male and female annual survival, with predicted annual survival peaking at intermediate genetic values for individual ACC. We also found weak evidence that males with intermediate genetic values had higher annual recruit production, but no evidence of a relationship with reproductive success in females. Altogether these results suggest potential stabilizing selection against extreme values of ACC, especially in male house sparrows.

\subsubsection*{Conclusions}
We demonstrate that individual variation in recombination rates influences fitness in a wild house sparrow population, with evidence of stabilizing selection on ACC particularly in male house sparrows. To interpret this association, we consider possible underlying biological mechanisms. On the methodological side, we present genomic prediction as a tool to address sampling biases. This novel approach allowed us to investigate the fitness consequences of a trait that can only be directly measured in reproductively successful individuals and allowed us to include several thousand individuals in the analysis that would otherwise have been excluded.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{House sparrow dataset}

We based our analyses on data from a metapopulation of house sparrows in a coastal archipelago in Helgeland, Northern Norway \citep{jensen2013genetic, ranke2021spatial}.
Since 1993, selected island-populations of house sparrows in the archipelago have been studied at the individual level.
Here, we used fitness data from $12$ islands, including adult annual reproductive success and survival data, collected during 1998--2019, and nestling recruitment data collected during 2007--2014 \citep{saatoglu2025metapopulation}. % unavailable... 
Upon first capture, sparrows were marked with unique rings, allowing their identification in future observations or recaptures.
If a sparrow was a nestling or fledgling at first capture, the hatch year was known to be the current year.
For individuals first captured as adults, the hatch year was assumed to be the current year if capture occurred in September or later, or the previous year if it occurred in August or earlier \citep{niskanen2020consistent, araya2021variation}. % TODO: double check
Hatch island was known for individuals observed as nestlings and juveniles, and was assumed to be the island of first observation for adults \citep[around $17\%$ of recruits dispersed during their first fall, see][]{ranke2021spatial}.
A 25 $\mu$L blood sample was taken from the brachial vein of each sparrow at first capture.
The blood samples were used to generate single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) data with an Axiom 200K SNP array \citep{lundregan2018inferences, niskanen2020consistent}, or a cross-compatible Axiom 70K SNP array containing a subset of the 200K array markers \citep{burnett2026snps}.
The SNP data set and ecological data were used to generate a genetic pedigree containing over $10\ 000$ individuals, as well as $F_\text{ROH}$ inbreeding coefficients for each individual \citep[see][for details]{burnett2026snps}.

\subsection*{Outline of fitness analyses}

We investigated how the number of autosomal crossovers impacted fitness in our study populations.
We addressed this by asking two related questions: first, how the ACC in the gametes an individual received from its parents impacted its fitness, and second, how an individual's own recombination rate impacted its fitness. % through the gametes passed along to the next generation.
In both analyses, we used mixed modeling to relate ACC to fitness components, including annual reproductive success (ARS), annual adult survival (AS), and nestling survival (NS).
However, the second analysis was complicated by sampling biases, which we accounted for by applying genomic prediction (GP) and measurement error modeling.
We fitted all models separately for the sexes since male and female ACC in house sparrows are essentially different traits with low genetic correlations \citep{mcauley2024genetic}, and since fitness mechanisms could differ between the sexes \note{(cite some house sparrow fitness papers)}.

\subsection*{Fitness impacts of ACC in inherited gametes}

We related the ACC in an individual's maternal and paternal gametes to that individual's (sex-specific) fitness using generalized linear mixed models for ARS, AS and NS, fitted in a Bayesian framework using \texttt{R-INLA} \citep{rue2009approximate}.
The ACC information was incorporated in two different ways. 
In what we label the ``sire and dam'' (S\&D) models, the ACCs in the gametes from the sire and dam were treated as two separate fixed effects in the same model, while in the ``parental sum'' (PS) models we used the sum of the ACC in the sire's and dam's gametes as a single covariate.
The S\&D models therefore allowed for the possibility that the egg-derived and sperm-derived ACCs have different fitness effects, with these effects estimated conditional on each other.
Conversely, the PS models considered the effect of overall inherited ACC, irrespective of source parent.
We used the fitted S\&D and PS models to predict sex-specific fitness components for given values of parental ACC, given that remaining fixed effect covariates were set to their average values.
Below we outline the S\&D and PS models for the three fitness traits.

\subsubsection*{Annual reproductive success}

ARS was modeled with a zero-inflated Poisson likelihood, so that $\text{ARS}_{ij}$, the number of recruits produced by individual $i$ in its $j^\text{th}$ adult year, was given by
\begin{align} % Zero-inflated Poisson GLMM
\begin{split}
\label{eq-ars}
\mathrm{ARS}_{ij} &\sim
\begin{cases}
0, & \text{with probability } \pi,\\[-10pt]
\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_{ij}), & \text{with probability } 1-\pi,
\end{cases}
\\[-5pt]
\operatorname{logit}(\pi) &= \gamma_0,
\\[-10pt]
\log(\lambda_{ij}) &= \eta_{ij},
\end{split}
\end{align}
where $\lambda_{ij}$ is the Poisson rate linked to the linear predictor $\eta_{ij}$ by a log link, and $\pi$ is the zero-inflation probability determined by a single intercept term $\gamma_0$.
For both the S\&D and PS models, $\eta_{ij}$ included an intercept, fixed effects for age (linear and quadratic) and $F_\text{ROH}$ inbreeding coefficient (linear), and unstructured random effects of measurement year, measurement island, individual ID, dam, and sire.
In the S\&D models, $\eta_{ij}$ also contained fixed effects of ACC in the dam gamete (linear and quadratic), ACC in the sire gamete (linear and quadratic). 
In the PS models, $\eta_{ij}$ instead included the sum of the ACCs in the two parental gametes (linear and quadratic).
Allowing for quadratic effects of ACC made it possible to detect potential stabilizing selection on recombination rate.
For the age covariate, we used an orthogonal polynomial basis for the linear and quadratic term, due to high correlation between age and squared age.
All fixed effect covariates were standardized before fitting the model.
For the count component intercept we used a $\N{\log\left(\overline{\mathrm{ARS}^{+}}\right)}{0.2^2}$ prior, where $\log\left(\overline{\mathrm{ARS}^{+}}\right)$ is the log-mean of the non-zero counts.
We placed independent $\N{0}{0.2^2}$ priors on the coefficients of the fixed effects, except a tighter $\N{0}{0.2^2/2}$ prior on the quadratic effect of ACC.
Independent penalized complexity priors $\mathrm{PC}(0.6, 0.05)$ were placed on the random effect standard deviations \citep{simpson2017penalising}.
Each $\mathrm{PC}$ prior can be interpreted as assigning a prior probability of $0.05$ to the corresponding random effect standard deviation exceeding $0.6$, and gives it an expected value of $0.2$.
Thus, our priors gave both fixed and random effects an expected range of $\pm 2\times0.2$ on the linear predictor scale, which translates to a multiplicative factor of $\exp(2\times0.2)\approx 1.5$ on the count scale, with the random effects unlikely to increase the count component by a multiplicative factor greater than $\exp(2\times0.6)\approx 3.3$.
For $\gamma_0$ we used a Gaussian prior $\N{0}{1.25^2}$, which we parametrized so that the zero-inflation probability $\pi$ was centered on $0.5$ with a roughly uniform shape between $0$ and $1$.

\subsubsection*{Annual Survival}

We modeled AS as a binomial trait with a logistic model, so that
\begin{align}
\begin{split}
\label{eq-as}
\mathrm{AS}_{ij} &\sim
\begin{cases}
0, & \text{with probability } 1 - p_{ij},\\[-10pt]
1, & \text{with probability } p_{ij},
\end{cases}
\\[-5pt]
\operatorname{logit}(p_{ij}) &= \eta_{ij},
\end{split}
\end{align}
where $\mathrm{AS}_{ij}$ is an indicator variable for whether individual $i$ survived its $j^\text{th}$ adult year, and $\eta_{ij}$ is a linear predictor linked to the survival probability $p_{ij}$ by a logit link.
For both the S\&D and PS models, we included the same fixed and random effects in $\eta_{ij}$ as in the model for ARS described above.
The prior specifications were also similar, but we adjusted the parameters for the logistic scale.
The intercept was given a $\N{\operatorname{logit}(\overline{\mathrm{AS}})}{0.5^2}$ prior, the coefficients of the fixed effects were given $\N{0}{0.5^2}$ priors, the quadratic effect of ACC was given a $\N{0}{0.5^2/2}$ prior, and the random effect standard deviations were given $\mathrm{PC}(1.5, 0.05)$ priors, which have an expected value of $0.5$.
In other words, effects were expected to vary over a $\pm 2 \times 0.5$ range on the linear predictor scale.
The probability range covered by this interval depends on where the interval is centered; for example, it corresponds to a range of $0.27$ to $0.73$ when centered on $\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(0)=0.5$.
Using the same centering, the PC priors implied that random effects were considered unlikely to shift probabilities outside the interval from $\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(-2\times1.5)\approx0.05$ to $\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(2\times1.5)\approx0.95$.

\subsubsection*{Nestling survival}

As with AS, NS was modeled as a binomial trait, so that
\begin{align}
\begin{split}
\label{eq-ns}
\mathrm{NS}_{i} &\sim
\begin{cases}
0, & \text{with probability } 1 - p_{i},\\[-10pt]
1, & \text{with probability } p_{i},
\end{cases}
\\[-5pt]
\operatorname{logit}(p_{i}) &= \eta_{i},
\end{split}
\end{align}
where $\mathrm{NS}_i$ is an indicator of whether nestling $i$ survived, and $\eta_i$ is a linear predictor linked to the survival probability $p_i$ by a logit link.
Like for ARS and AS, we defined the S\&D and PS models for NS by including linear and quadratic fixed effects of either the ACC inherited from each parent (S\&D) or the sum of the ACC from the two parents (PS), respectively.
The linear predictor for NS additionally included an intercept, fixed effects of $F_\text{ROH}$ inbreeding coefficient (linear), hatch date (day of year, linear and quadratic), and age at first capture (days, linear), as well as unstructured random effects of hatch year, hatch island, clutch ID, dam, and sire.
We used the same prior specifications for NS as for AS, as both traits were modeled on the logit scale, with the tighter $\N{0}{0.5^2/2}$ prior on the quadratic effects of both ACC and hatch date.

\subsection*{Fitness impacts of own recombination rates}

\subsubsection*{Sampling bias in ACC measurements}

To investigate the fitness consequences of an individual's recombination rate, we could in principle have related the ACCs in the gametes produced by individual $i$ directly to the fitness of $i$.
However, since we were only able to measure ACC for genotyped individuals \textit{with genotyped offspring}, any individuals that were reproductively unsuccessful were unmeasured for ACC.
Thus, a fitness analysis excluding these individuals would suffer from a systematic sampling bias, where less fit individuals would be missing not at random.
As a workaround for this sampling bias, we used GP to predict additive genetic values (also known as breeding values) of ACC for \textit{all} genotyped individuals with fitness data, including non-breeders.
By considering the fitness effects of the predicted additive genetic component of ACC, rather than the unevenly sampled ACC measurements, we eliminated the sampling bias.
This approach essentially uses GP as a tool for imputation of the missing ACC phenotypes.

Below we outline a Bayesian GP model that estimated and predicted sex-specific ACC genetic values. 
We used these genetic values as covariates in fitness models for ARS, AS, and NS, which we label ``genetic value-fitness'' (GV-F) models to differentiate them from the S\&D and PS models.
Ideally, the GP and GV-F models would have been fitted jointly, but because this was computationally infeasible, we took care to propagate the uncertainty from the GP results into the GV-F models.
Similar to the GV-F models, we also related \textit{parental} genetic values, in what we label ``parental genetic value-fitness'' (PGV-F) models.
As with the S\&D and PS models, we used the fitted (P)GV-F models to predict sex-specific fitness components for given (parental) genetic values, when the remaining fixed effect covariates were set to their average values.
The GP, GV-F, and PGV-F models, which are outlined below, were fitted separately for males and females.

\subsubsection*{Genomic Prediction of ACC}

We predicted additive genetic values for ACC with a GP model.
The GP model was a Bayesian linear mixed model with a Gaussian likelihood, fitted in \texttt{R-INLA} \citep{rue2009approximate}.
Although ACC is a count trait, its values were high enough that a continuous Gaussian likelihood was a reasonable modeling assumption.
Let $\mathrm{ACC}_{ij}$ denote the ACC measured in the gamete that individual $i$ passed to its offspring $j$, where the number of measurements per individual varied from $0$ (missing) to $42$.
We modeled
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{ACC}_{ij} = \mu + \beta_\text{coverage} \cdot x_{\text{coverage},ij} + \beta_f f_i +  g_i + z_{\text{hy},i} + z_{\text{hi},i} + z_{\text{id},i} + z_{\varepsilon,ij}\ ,
    \label{eq-gp}
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is an intercept, $\beta_\text{coverage}$ is the regression coefficient for total coverage $x_{\text{coverage},ij}$, $\beta_f$ is the regression coefficient for $F_\text{ROH}$ value $f_i$ of individual $i$ and $z_{\text{hy},i}$, $z_{\text{hi},i}$, $z_{\text{id},i}$, and $z_{\varepsilon,ij}$ are unstructured random effects corresponding to hatch year, (known or presumed) hatch island, permanent environmental effects and residual environmental effects, respectively.
Finally, $g_i$ is a structured random effect denoting the genetic value of individual $i$.
The genetic value vector $\bm g = \left(g_1, g_2, \ldots, g_N\right)^\top$ follows the distribution $\N{\bm{0}}{\sigma_{\text{G}}^2 \mathbf{G}}$, where $\sigma_{\text{G}}^2$ is the additive genetic variance for ACC and $\mathbf{G}$ is a pre-computed genomic relatedness matrix as given by \citet{vanraden2008efficient}. % estimated with the first of the formulas given by \citet{vanraden2008efficient}. % note to self if later writing out the GRM - p_i has been used for survival probabilities
The genetic value $g_i$ represents the genetic propensity of individual $i$ to produce gametes with high ACC relative to the overall population, and thus reflects the relative recombination rate of that individual.
We placed independent normal priors $\N{0}{10^4}$ on the fixed effect coefficients, and penalized complexity priors $\mathrm{PC}\left(\hat \sigma / \sqrt{2}, 0.05\right)$ on the random effect variances \citep{simpson2017penalising}, where $\hat\sigma$ was the sample standard deviation of $\mathrm{ACC}_{ij}$. % todo: update priors
Fitting the Bayesian GP model \eqref{eq-gp} yielded a joint posterior distribution for the genetic value vector $\bm g$.
This posterior was approximately multivariate Gaussian (results not shown), and we summarized it with the sample mean and covariance matrix obtained from $10^4$ posterior samples.
The resulting Gaussian approximation for the distribution of $\bm g$ was used in the subsequent GV-F models.

\subsubsection*{Fitness analyses using genetic values}

We fitted models for ARS, AS and NS that used the posterior estimates of the genetic values $g_i$ obtained from the GP in Equation \eqref{eq-gp} as the covariate of interest.
These GV-F models were Bayesian GLMMs with the same form as the previously described S\&D and PS models in Equations \eqref{eq-ars}, \eqref{eq-as} and \eqref{eq-ns}, respectively.
The GV-F models differed from the S\&D and PS models in that we removed the sire and dam random effects for the two adult fitness traits (ARS and AS), and for all three fitness traits we replaced the ACC covariates with linear and quadratic effects of $g_i$.
This replacement was not straightforward, since $g_i$ was estimated with uncertainty in GP models, and this uncertainty should be propagated into the GV-F models.
However, the measurement-error methods available in R-INLA clash with our need to include the square of $g_i$ as a covariate to allow detection of stabilizing selection.
Adding this quadratic term would result in a non-Gaussian linear predictor in the fitness models, which violates a core assumption of \texttt{R-INLA}.
To retain the squared term while also accounting for the uncertainty in $g_i$, we therefore fitted the GV-F models using MCMC as implemented in \texttt{RStan} \citep{rstan}.
In the MCMC we treated $g_i$ as an unknown latent variable whose prior corresponds to the Gaussian approximation of the posterior distribution of $\bm g$ inferred from the GP.
This approach encoded both the uncertainty and the covariance structure among the genetic values $g_i$ as prior information in the GV-F models, while still allowing the fitness data to further update the distribution of $\bm g$.
In other words, instead of fixing $g_i$, we treated $\bm g$ as a latent variable which was updated in each MCMC iteration, and used the latent $\bm g$ as a covariate with linear and quadratic effects.
Since we simultaneously updated $g_i$ and estimated its effect on fitness, the procedure can be regarded as an example of ``feedback'' \citep{plummer2015cuts}.
Although such feedback can sometimes bias results, so we deemed it appropriate here since a permutation analysis with $250$ replicates showed no indication that the method produced false-positive results (\note{details to come in suppmat}).
% Permutation analyses showed that this approach did not produce false-positive results (see SXX).
Each MCMC model consisted of $16$ chains which ran for $48\ 000$ iterations, including $8\ 000$ burn-in iterations.
To conserve storage memory, we retained every $160^\text{th}$ iteration, resulting in a total of $4\ 000$ posterior samples per model.
We used the default \texttt{RStan} settings, except we set \texttt{adapt\_delta = 0.96} to mitigate divergent transitions.
% And all models converged with good mixing according to rstan.
All GV-F models used the same priors that were used in the S\&D and PS models for the same fitness trait.

We also related an individual's fitness to their same-sex parent's genetic value for ACC, in models that we label \textit{parental genetic value-fitness} models (PGV-F).
The PGV-F were analogous to the S\&D and PS models in that they investigated the relationship between a parent's recombination rate and its offspring's fitness, while also using the techniques from the GV-F models to eliminate a potential sampling bias.
% why is there a sampling bias?
To this end, we formulated the PGV-F model for a given fitness trait like the identically the S\&D and PS model, but with the ACC covariate(s) replaced by the parental additive genetic value $g_{p(i)}$, where $p(i)$ denotes the same-sex parent of individual $i$.
The covariate $g_{p(i)}$ was treated with the same measurement error model as used in GV-F, and so the PGV-F models were also fitted using \texttt{RStan}.
